# Concepts Shared by Most Global Anti-Bribery Laws

Most major global anti-bribery frameworks — including the **US Foreign Corrupt Practices Act (FCPA)**, the **UK Bribery Act 2010**, the **OECD Anti-Bribery Convention**, the **UN Convention Against Corruption (UNCAC)**, the **French Sapin II Law**, and the **Brazil Clean Company Act** — share the following core concepts:

---

## 1. Prohibition of Bribery (Active and Passive)
- **Active bribery**: offering, promising, or giving a bribe.
- **Passive bribery**: requesting, accepting, or receiving a bribe.
- Both sides of the transaction are typically prohibited.

## 2. Coverage of Public Officials
- All major laws explicitly prohibit bribing **domestic and/or foreign public officials** (government employees, politicians, officials of state-owned enterprises, etc.).
- The definition of "public official" is broad in most frameworks.

## 3. Private-Sector / Commercial Bribery
- Increasingly, laws also cover bribery **between private parties** (business-to-business), not just involving government officials.
- The UK Bribery Act 2010 is particularly broad in this regard.

## 4. Corporate / Organizational Liability
- Companies and legal entities (not only individuals) can be held **criminally or civilly liable** for bribery committed by their employees, agents, or subsidiaries.
- Liability can arise even without proof of knowledge by senior management ("strict liability" or "failure to prevent" approaches).

## 5. Adequate Procedures / Compliance Defense
- Most laws allow organizations to use an **"adequate procedures"** or **"compliance program"** defense.
- If a company demonstrates it had robust anti-bribery controls, it may avoid or reduce liability.
- Key elements: risk assessments, policies, training, due diligence on third parties, monitoring, and auditing.

## 6. Third-Party / Intermediary Liability
- Companies can be liable for bribes paid **through agents, consultants, distributors, or joint-venture partners** acting on their behalf.
- Due diligence on third parties is a critical compliance requirement.

## 7. Extraterritorial Reach
- Most modern anti-bribery laws apply **beyond national borders**.
- The FCPA applies to US-listed companies and US persons worldwide; the UK Bribery Act applies to any company with a UK nexus.
- This means multinational companies must comply regardless of where the bribery occurs.

## 8. Books, Records, and Internal Controls
- Laws such as the FCPA include explicit requirements for **accurate accounting records** and **adequate internal controls**.
- Falsifying accounts to conceal bribes is itself a violation.

## 9. Facilitation Payments
- Small payments to expedite routine government actions ("grease payments") are treated inconsistently:
  - **FCPA**: has a narrow exception for facilitation payments.
  - **UK Bribery Act**: prohibits them outright.
- The global trend is toward prohibition of facilitation payments.

## 10. Gifts, Hospitality, and Entertainment
- Most frameworks regulate (or prohibit) **gifts, travel, meals, and entertainment** given to officials or business partners.
- The key test is whether the gift is **reasonable, proportionate, and transparent**, and not intended to influence a decision.

## 11. Whistleblower Protections and Reporting Mechanisms
- Many laws require or incentivize the creation of **internal reporting channels**.
- Whistleblowers who report bribery are protected from retaliation under most modern frameworks.

## 12. Sanctions and Penalties
- Both **criminal** (imprisonment, fines) and **civil** (disgorgement of profits, civil penalties) sanctions apply.
- Penalties can be very large — fines in FCPA cases, for example, can reach hundreds of millions of dollars.

## 13. Non-Deductibility of Bribes
- Most jurisdictions prohibit companies from **deducting bribe payments** as business expenses for tax purposes.

## Key Takeaway

The **core shared concepts** across most global anti-bribery laws are:
1. Prohibiting both giving and receiving bribes
2. Holding corporations liable (not just individuals)
3. Covering third-party intermediaries
4. Applying extraterritorially
5. Requiring accurate books and records
6. Providing a compliance program defense
7. Imposing significant criminal and civil penalties
